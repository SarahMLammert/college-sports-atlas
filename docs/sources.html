College sports

About the data

Rosters scraped from each school’s official athletics site.

Every dot on these maps, in every sport, is a player on a real college roster. Here’s how those rosters were collected, what’s counted, and where the gaps are.

i.The rosters

Every player comes from a school’s own official athletics website, the roster page each team posts every season. Nothing comes from recruiting services, star ratings or paid databases. Every sport is collected the same way.

Each roster page gives a player’s name, position, class year, hometown and, usually, high school and previous college. A program collects those pages for every school and every season, including each site’s archived rosters from past years.

  • Each archived page is checked to make sure it really is that season. Some sites quietly show this year’s roster for every year, and those pages aren’t used.
  • Pages are downloaded slowly, with pauses between requests, so no school’s site gets overloaded.
  • Sites that turn away automated visitors are left out, not worked around.
  • Coaches and staff are removed. Names are cleaned of jersey numbers and captain marks.
  • Each sport has its own list of schools, with the years each program played and the division it played in each year, so a school that moved up a level is counted at the right level every season.

iii.Putting hometowns on the map

Each hometown as listed on the roster (“Bastrop, TX”) is matched to a place and given a latitude and longitude. A dot is drawn for each town, sized by how many players came from there. Every sport uses the same list of places.

  • An outlined dot means the location is approximate: the town could only be matched to its county, or to a town with the same name.
  • A town given without a state (“Pleasant Grove”) is placed in the state that player’s school recruits from most.
  • Misspelled hometowns are fixed by hand from a checked list, so “Douglasvillle” lands on Douglasville.
  • Players from outside the U.S. appear on the world map.

iv.Counting transfers

In every sport, a player counts as a transfer when any of these is true:

  • The roster names a previous college.
  • The player appears on another school’s roster in an earlier season, with the same name and hometown.
  • The player is new to a team but already a junior, senior or graduate student.

The Transfer Map only draws a line when both schools are in that sport’s data, so the school the player left is known. Other transfers are counted by the kind of school they came from: Division I, Division II, Division III, NAIA, junior college or unknown.

A player who transfers appears once for each school they played for.

v.Known gaps

  • Missing seasons. Some schools’ sites don’t have every old roster, so those seasons undercount (in football, Ohio State 2016, Samford 2016–17 and Yale 2016, for example).
  • Missing schools. Smaller colleges are more often missing: some sites refuse automated visitors, some post rosters only as PDFs, and a few programs have no athletics site at all.
  • Missing hometowns. A few sites, mostly junior colleges, leave the hometown blank. Those players are counted but have no dot on the map.
  • Wrong class years. Some archives show each player’s final class instead of that season’s. Those seasons are found and handled so freshmen aren’t counted as transfers.
  • Unlisted previous schools. Many rosters never name a player’s previous college, so a few transfers are missed.
  • Unmatched moves. Some transfers name a school that’s in the data but couldn’t be matched to its roster, usually because a name or hometown is spelled differently. They’re counted but don’t have a line on the Transfer Map yet (in football, about 6,000 of them).
  • Same-name high schools. Schools like “Central” are told apart only by the player’s home state.

vi.Maps

Country and state outlines come from Natural Earth and the U.S. Census Bureau, through the open-source world-atlas and us-atlas packages. The maps are drawn with D3.
